# Chleomancer

## Section One: The Tale of Antichliarios

### 1.1 The Woman Who Listens

Antichliarios was old enough that the younger ones had stopped counting her winters. Her hands were what the band valued: thick-knuckled, patient, certain. She could look at a piece of flint and see the blade already living inside it, could feel through her palm exactly where a stone wanted to be struck and where it would only shatter. When she worked, others gathered to watch, not because they needed her finished tools but because the process itself held something they could not name.

What none of them noticed, or noticed only as a habit of an old woman grown strange with age, was that Antichliarios listened to her work as much as she looked at it. A scraper struck one way rang thin and quick. The same stone struck differently, held differently, gave a duller sound that died faster in the air. She began testing this the way she tested an edge, by repetition, by memory, by comparing one strike against the last. The others saw a craftswoman with sensitive hands. They did not see that she had started to keep two kinds of knowledge at once: how a stone cuts, and how a stone sounds.

### 1.2 Cords from the Marsh

In the wet months she worked cattail stems into cord, rolling the fibers against her thigh until they twisted tight enough to hold a hafted point through a season of use. It was tedious, necessary work, the kind that filled the hands while the mind wandered.

One evening, waiting for a length of cord to finish drying, she tied a small worked point to its end and let it hang. The wind moved it. It struck the side of a log she had been hollowing into a trough for rendering fat, and it rang.

She had heard a thousand accidental sounds in her life. This one she did not let go. She tied another point to a shorter cord, and another to a longer one, and hung all three from a branch above the trough, and sat with them for the better part of an evening, moving them, listening to what changed and what stayed the same. The short cord swung fast and struck twice before the long cord had swung once; shortening or lengthening it changed how often the sound repeated, not what the sound was. What changed the sound itself was something else: a heavier point against the trough's thick base gave a low tone that held in the air, while a thin point struck near the rim gave something high that vanished almost at once. She had, without a name for either one, discovered two things at once that most people would have called the same thing: how often a sound returned, and what a sound was made of. The cord gave her the first. The stone and the strike gave her the second.

### 1.3 The Spinning Stones

The binding of tools was practical work, the kind a life depended on. Antichliarios turned it, in private hours, into something else. She learned that if she twisted a hung point and let it go, it would spin and untwist and spin the other way, striking the log twice for every release, and that the spacing between those two strikes could be lengthened or shortened depending on how tightly she had wound it.

She began repeating sequences. Not by accident now, but by intention: three quick strikes from a short cord, a pause, one low strike from a long one. She could do it again the next evening and produce something recognizably the same, not identical, but recognizably itself, the way a particular knapped edge was recognizably her work and not someone else's. She had found repetition without repetition of the same physical stroke. This was the part that later generations, trying to reconstruct what she had done, would find hardest to recover: not the stones, not even the cords, but the fact that she had learned to want the same pattern twice and had built a method for getting it.

### 1.4 The First Song

She performed it for the band on a night when the fire had burned low and there was little else to do but sit close together against the cold. She hung three points from a frame of branches lashed over the trough, and she moved among them, spinning, striking, pausing, letting the sounds layer and fade and layer again.

The band went quiet in a way they did not go quiet for stories or for warnings of predators. Some of the children reached toward the swinging cords and were pulled back by their mothers. An old hunter, who had seen Antichliarios work stone for forty years, watched her hands as though she had suddenly become someone he did not know.

Afterward no two people described what they had witnessed the same way. One said the spirits of the dead had spoken through the hanging stones. Another said Antichliarios had found a way to make the wind visible. A third, more practical, said only that she had built something clever with cord and refused to be more impressed than that. There was no word in their speech for what had happened, so each listener reached for the nearest word they already owned and made the event fit inside it.

### 1.5 The Broken Inheritance

Antichliarios died two winters later, of nothing dramatic, only age catching up with a body that had worked stone for sixty years. She had tried hardest with a grandnephew named Idu, patient enough to sit through an evening of demonstration but not patient enough to sit through a season of failure. He could copy her strikes well enough, could hang a point and spin it, but when she asked him why a heavy point against the trough's base gave a different sound than a thin one near the rim, he had no interest in the question, only in whether he had made a sound at all. After a few tries he decided, without unkindness, that his aunt's hobby was simply a thing she alone could do, the way certain elders had a particular laugh that no one else could quite reproduce either. She watched him lose interest and understood, before anyone said it aloud to her, that what she had found would very likely die with her. Not for lack of anyone trying to keep it, but because trying once or twice was not the same as staying with a small thing long enough to hear what it wanted to say next.

She had shown three or four others besides Idu how to hang a point and set it spinning. None of them had watched closely enough, or practiced long enough, or cared enough beyond the novelty of the thing, to keep the whole method alive. The frames rotted. The cords were reused for other bindings, as cord always was, because cord was precious and a song was not obviously worth more than a spare length of rope.

What survived her was fragments: a story told badly, about an old woman who made the wind sing. A gesture, half-remembered, of an arm spinning something invisible. A vague unease, in certain families, about hanging things from branches near the fire. The discovery had been real. It had also been, in the way of most first discoveries, nearly impossible to hand forward whole. What Antichliarios had known could not be simply told to the next generation. It had to be found again, by someone else, willing to notice something small and stay with it long enough to hear what it was trying to say.

## Section Two: The Curious Wind

### 2.1 Breath from the Earth

Generations had passed, enough that Antichliarios was no longer a person anyone remembered, only a word attached to an unreliable story about wind and singing. Tak was a young man with no particular gift for stone or for hunting, the kind of person a band tolerates rather than depends on, and he had a habit that made him mildly irritating to be around: he noticed things that did not matter.

He noticed, one late-summer afternoon, that a narrow crack in a limestone outcrop was breathing cold air on a day when the rock around it had been sitting in sun since morning. Everyone who passed that crack felt the same cool breath and thought nothing further of it, the way a person feels a draft in a house and does not ask where the house is open to the sky. Tak knelt by it and held his hand there and thought: air does not come from nowhere. Somewhere behind this crack there is a space large enough to hold air that has not yet been warmed by the day.

### 2.2 The First Excavation

He said as much to an older cousin, who laughed and told him that plenty of holes breathed cold air and none of them led anywhere worth the effort of finding out. Tak began digging anyway, in the hours after his other obligations were done, scraping at the crack with an antler pick and his bare hands.

Progress was almost nothing. A handful of loosened stone a day, sometimes less. The crack widened by the width of a finger over the course of a month. Anyone watching would have called it a poor use of a young man's strength, and several people did say so, not unkindly, the way one points out an obvious waste.

### 2.3 Seasons of Stone

The digging went on for years. Not continuously, not obsessively in a way that stopped him from hunting or gathering or taking a mate, but persistently, in the margins of a life that had other demands. He was hurt twice, once by a rockfall that bruised his hip badly enough to keep him from walking well for a season, once by a blade of stone that opened his palm to the bone.

The hole became a known feature of the landscape, the way a lightning-struck tree becomes known. People pointed it out to visitors as an example of Tak's particular strangeness. Children were sometimes sent to peer into it and report that it still went nowhere. Tak kept digging. He could not have said, if asked, exactly why the cold breath still meant something to him after so many seasons of finding only more rock. It simply had not stopped meaning something.

### 2.4 The Passage

In the seventh year the pick broke through into open space, and cold air rushed out hard enough to make him flinch back from the hole. He widened it enough to fit his shoulders, then his hips, and on a morning he told no one about in advance, in case he was wrong, he pushed himself through a passage barely wider than his body, in total darkness, with a torch he could not yet light because both his hands were needed for the crawl.

The passage narrowed twice to the point where he thought he would have to turn back, his chest scraping stone above and below, before it opened all at once into a space his torch, lit now and held ahead of him, could not find the edges of.

### 2.5 The Chamber

The cave was larger than any enclosed space he had stood inside in his life. His torchlight caught pale stone formations hanging from the ceiling like frozen rain, pools of still black water, the pale bones of something long dead near one wall, claw marks scored deep into a slope of hardened clay where bears had used the chamber long before him and might still use it now.

Sound behaved strangely here. His own breathing came back to him from directions he could not account for. A dropped stone did not simply fall and stop; it fell, and struck, and the strike continued outward and returned changed, layered over itself, taking longer to die than it had any right to.

### 2.6 The Stone That Answers

He struck one of the hanging formations without meaning to, steadying himself as he climbed past it, and the note it gave off was unlike anything a stone had ever done in his hands before: sustained, clear, holding in the air long after his hand had moved on. He struck it again, deliberately this time, and then struck others nearby, and found that each one answered differently, some dull, some ringing, some producing almost no sound at all, depending on their shape and thickness and how they hung from the rock above.

He sat for a long time in the dark, striking one formation and then another, arranging a rough sequence of sounds he could repeat, the way a much older woman whose name he had never heard had once arranged a sequence of sounds from three points hung on cattail cord. He had found, without knowing it, the same thing twice removed: that certain shapes, certain suspensions, certain arrangements of stone and air and impact, produced something a person could return to and would recognize as itself. He climbed back out into daylight already trying to find the words to tell the others what he had found, and already, though he did not yet know it, failing.

## Section Three: A Truth Without Passage

### 3.1 A Report Without a Witness

He tried to tell it in order, the way it had happened: the cold breath, the years of digging, the narrow crawl, the chamber larger than any space he had stood in, the stones that answered when struck. Said all together, in one telling, it did not sound like a report. It sounded like the kind of story told around a fire to frighten children, each new claim less believable than the last, until a hidden room full of singing rock sat on top of seven years of digging in a hole everyone already knew about and had already decided meant nothing.

He watched their faces change as he spoke, watched belief curdle into something more like concern. He had spent a third of his life reaching this discovery. He could not compress the seven years of patience into the sentence that would make the singing stones sound plausible rather than invented.

### 3.2 The Failure of Imitation

Someone asked him to show them, and he agreed at once, relieved to have a way forward that did not depend on being believed on his word alone. He struck stones from the riverbed in front of the gathered band, hoping to produce even a rough approximation of what he had heard underground.

The stones gave dull, ordinary sounds, the sounds any struck stone gives. He tried larger ones, flatter ones, stones he held suspended by a cord in imitation of what he half-remembered Antichliarios's descendants still occasionally mentioned. Nothing rang the way the cave stones had rung. Each failed attempt did more damage to his claim than silence would have done. A man who says he saw something extraordinary and then cannot show even a fragment of it looks, to everyone watching, less like an honest witness and more like someone caught in the middle of an exaggeration.

### 3.3 Familiar Explanations

The band did not reject him cruelly. They did what people do with an account that does not fit anything they already know: they translated it into something that did fit. Some said he had heard an echo, amplified strangely by rock, and mistaken it for singing. Some said seven years alone in a hole, digging toward nothing, could unsettle anyone's mind, and that what he called a chamber of singing stones was closer to a fever dream than a place. A few, less generously, said he had simply decided that after so many years of being laughed at he needed a story large enough to justify the digging, and had built one.

Each explanation let its holder keep the world exactly as they had already understood it. None of them required entering the passage themselves.

### 3.4 The Cost of Insistence

Tak did not have the temperament to let it go quietly. He repeated the account, added detail he hoped would help, described the exact ring of a particular formation, the way sound in the chamber seemed to arrive from more directions than it had left. The more precisely he tried to describe it, the more it read, to people who had not been there, as elaboration rather than accuracy.

His standing in the band, never especially high, dropped further. People who had once tolerated his strangeness with mild amusement began to speak of him more carefully, the way one speaks of someone whose judgment can no longer quite be trusted.

### 3.5 The Refused Journey

He asked, eventually, if anyone would come with him and see it directly. A handful considered it. The passage was narrow enough that a person twice his size would not fit through it at all, and Tak was not a large man. The floor of the crawl showed no daylight for a distance no one could measure from outside. There were claw marks, he had told them himself, evidence that something large used the chamber, or had used it, and no one could say which.

Weighed against seven years of watching Tak dig a hole that led nowhere anyone had confirmed, the risk of the crawl did not seem worth taking. The one piece of evidence that could have settled the matter completely, his own witnessed presence in the chamber, was exactly the piece no one was willing to acquire for themselves.

### 3.6 Evidence That Cannot Travel

Alone again at the mouth of his hole, Tak understood the shape of the problem more clearly than he had words for. He had not found something he could describe his way out of. Language, on its own, was carrying almost none of what mattered: not the years of digging that made the discovery credible, not the acoustic strangeness of the chamber that made it worth discovering, not the danger that made other people's caution reasonable. What he had found could not be handed to anyone secondhand. It would have to be brought to them directly, whole, or it would have to be brought to them in some form small enough to carry through a passage that most of the band would never willingly enter.

## Section Four: Kythera

### 4.1 The Keeper of Fragments

There was a woman in the band, older than Tak by two decades, who occupied a role no one had a clean word for. Kythera kept the stories that had gone ragged with retelling, the half-remembered techniques nobody quite practiced anymore, the odd bits of craft that had outlived the reasons people first learned them. Her rule, rarely stated but never broken, was that a fragment should be kept exactly as it came to her rather than filled in or smoothed to make it more useful. She had watched too many old accounts get corrected by well-meaning retellers until nothing of the original remained inside the more convincing version that replaced it. She was the one who still knew, in fragments, the old account of a woman generations back who was said to have made the wind sing, and she guarded that fragment the way she guarded all the others: unfinished, uncertain, and untouched.

She did not laugh at Tak, and she did not immediately believe him either. She listened to the whole account twice, the second time asking him to describe the sound itself rather than the chamber around it, and something in his description of a struck stone ringing longer than it should have snagged against the oldest fragment she kept: an old woman, cords, spinning points, a sound the first listeners had no word for.

### 4.2 Belief Is Not Enough

Tak, encouraged that someone was finally taking him seriously, asked her directly to stand with him, to tell the others she believed his account. Kythera refused, in a way that stung more than outright dismissal would have.

She told him that her believing him changed nothing for anyone who had not heard the cave themselves. Sincerity was not evidence. A person could be entirely honest and entirely wrong, or entirely honest and simply unable to make others understand what they had experienced. If he wanted the band to accept what he had found, sincerity was the wrong currency. He needed something they could examine themselves, something that did not depend on trusting him.

There was something else beneath her caution that she did not say to him yet. If she helped him rebuild what the old fragment only gestured toward, she would be doing to Antichliarios's memory exactly what she had spent her life refusing to let anyone do to the accounts in her keeping. Filling a gap was not the same as preserving what stood on either side of it, and she had never before had to consider that the two might sometimes turn out to be the same act.

### 4.3 Reconstructing Antichliarios

She took him instead to the fragment she actually possessed: the half-remembered account of cattail cord and hanging points and rotation. Together they twisted cord from marsh reeds, hung worked points at different lengths, spun them, struck them against a hollow log the way the old story vaguely described. Most of their early attempts produced nothing but the ordinary knock of stone on wood.

Eventually, after many failed lengths and many failed points, they produced something unmistakably like what the fragment described: a note that lingered, a sequence that could be repeated on purpose rather than stumbled into by accident. It was modest next to what Tak remembered from the cave, but it was real, and it was theirs, made in the open where anyone could watch it happen.

Tak understood, watching the cord swing and the point strike and the sound hang in the evening air, that he and this unnamed woman generations dead had found different faces of the same thing. Stone, suspension, tension, motion, cavity. The cave had simply built these relations into its own rock at a scale neither of them could have arranged with their hands.

### 4.4 Lessons in Resonance

Kythera pressed him further than he wanted to be pressed. Why did the short cord ring higher than the long one. Why did some points sound and others merely thud. Why had the cave stones sung so freely while the riverbed stones he'd tried in front of the band had stayed stubbornly dull.

He did not have full answers, but forming the questions taught him to take apart what he had been calling one thing into several. There was timing: how quickly a sound repeated, set by the length of cord and the force of a spin. There was excitation: the strike itself, its force and exactly where it landed. There was resonance: whatever it was in a particular stone's shape and thickness that let it ring rather than thud when struck the same way. And there was enclosure: the space a sound traveled through afterward, which could carry a note outward and let it return, or simply let it die flat against open air. Four different things, easy to hear as a single impression and easy to lose entirely once a person tried to pull them apart.

### 4.5 The Disagreement

This understanding, once he had it, produced an idea that would undo the fragile peace between them. If resonance depended on how a stone's ends were held as much as on the stone itself, and if the cave's particular way of holding it could not be carried out of the ground, then the honest solution was to bring the stone anyway and find some new way of holding it.

He told Kythera he meant to remove one of the smaller singing stalactites and carry it back to the band. She pushed back harder than she had before, and not only because she doubted it would work. The stalactite was not, in her thinking, an object with a hidden voice waiting inside it. It was one end of a fixed connection, grown into the ceiling for longer than either of them could reckon, singing only because that connection held one end rigid while the tip below hung free to move. Break the connection and there was no guarantee that any new way of holding the stone would produce anything like the old sound, because they would not simply be missing a piece of an arrangement. They would be starting over with an object whose relationship to its own ends no longer existed in any form either of them understood.

And beneath the argument about resonance sat the older one she had not yet said aloud. Taking a whole, singing thing and breaking it on the chance of learning something from the pieces was exactly the kind of act she had spent her life guarding fragments against. She had watched what remained of Antichliarios's actual voice dwindle to a badly told story precisely because people had handled it carelessly, generation after generation, each one certain their small alteration would cost nothing. Tak was asking her to make that same kind of alteration herself, on purpose, to the one intact thing they had found.

### 4.6 A Dangerous Plan

Tak had turned the argument over enough times that he had an answer for her, even if it was not a comforting one. An imperfect demonstration that people could see and touch was worth more to him than a perfect experience that stayed forever locked behind a passage no one would enter. If extraction destroyed the stone's voice, he had at least tried the only path that did not depend entirely on being believed. If some fragment of the sound survived the removal, or could be coaxed back into it afterward, the band would finally have something they could examine for themselves rather than take on his word.

Kythera did not agree that the risk was worth taking. But she recognized, watching him, that he had reached the point past which caution could not be argued into him, and that if the stone was coming out of that cave regardless, it would come out with someone present who understood, even partially, what made it sing in the first place.

It cost her something to say yes. She had spent her life keeping fragments from being disturbed any further than they already had been, and here she was agreeing to help break a whole, singing thing into a fragment on purpose, in the hope that a fragment carried out into the light was worth more than a whole thing left forever in the dark. She was not certain, even as she agreed, that she believed this. She went anyway.

## Section Five: Catharsis

### 5.1 Choosing the Stone

They returned to the cave with two others Kythera had persuaded, both younger, both strong enough to help carry weight through the crawl and both sworn to say nothing until Tak was ready to show what they brought back. Inside, by torchlight, Tak and Kythera moved among the hanging formations, striking each in turn, arguing in whispers about which one could be spared.

They settled on a stalactite near the chamber's outer wall, smaller than the ones deeper in, its note clear but not the richest they had heard. It hung where its removal would not obviously threaten the formations around it, and it was, as much as anything in that cave could be, replaceable to no one but the four of them and whatever the cave had been before any human entered it.

### 5.2 Cutting the Cave

The work took most of a day. They drove dry wedges of hardened wood into the hairline cracks already present in the stone, then soaked the wedges with water carried in from the entrance pool. Dry wood drinks water and swells, and by the second round of soaking the cracks had visibly widened without a single blow needing to strike them again. Someone suggested packing coals against the base instead, to crack the stone with heat the way flint sometimes split when thrown into a fire, and Kythera refused outright: a fire in a space this enclosed could as easily bring down the ceiling around all of them as split the stone they wanted to keep whole. They worked leverage bars of green branch under the base where it joined the wall, patient rather than forceful, widening what the swelling wood had already started. Twice a crack ran further than intended and they stopped, certain they had ruined it, and found on inspection that the stone still held.

Kythera worked in near silence, checking each new fracture against her memory of the cord experiments, murmuring calculations Tak did not fully follow about weight and balance and where the stone's true center might sit once it was free of the rock holding it up.

### 5.3 The Great Removal

The stalactite came loose with a crack loud enough to echo through the whole chamber and startle all four of them into stillness, certain for a moment that something worse than a falling stone was coming down on them. Nothing followed. They lowered it on ropes of twisted hide, wrapped it in more hide to keep it from further fracturing, and began the slow work of moving it toward the passage.

The crawl that had barely admitted Tak's body years earlier now had to admit four people and a wrapped stone heavier than any of them wanted to say aloud. They passed it hand to hand, foot by foot, in total darkness at the narrowest point, and came out into daylight scraped, exhausted, and in possession of a piece of the cave that had never before existed outside it.

### 5.4 The Silent Stone

Word had already spread that Tak had gone back into the ground with Kythera and two others, and the band gathered before they had even finished unwrapping the stone. Tak set it as best he could in imitation of how it had hung underground, suspended from a crude frame by hide rope, and struck it in front of everyone.

It gave a flat, short knock, indistinguishable from any ordinary stone dropped on the ground. He struck it again, adjusting the angle, adjusting the point of impact, and got the same dead sound each time. Someone in the crowd said, not unkindly but not gently either, that they had risked their lives and spent a day cutting rock out of the earth to produce a noise any child could make with a handful of pebbles. The doubt that had followed Tak for years settled back over him heavier than before, because this time he had brought them the proof itself and the proof had failed in front of everyone.

### 5.5 The Remainder of Antichliarios

Kythera was the one who did not walk away disappointed. She crouched by the stone, turning it, running her hands along the break where it had separated from the ceiling, and said, half to herself, that its silence made a kind of sense once she considered what had actually changed. In the chamber the stone had been fixed at one end, held rigid where it joined the rock above, free only at its tip. What had vibrated there was a shape locked into that particular way of being held. Hanging loose now from a hide rope, free at both ends and rigid at neither, it was not failing to make its old sound. It was a different vibrating thing entirely, made of the same stone but no longer shaped, at its ends, the way the cave had shaped it.

That distinction mattered more than it first appeared to. They were not searching for the stone's hidden voice, waiting somewhere inside the material to be coaxed back out. There was no original sound trapped inside it, only a material capable of many different sounds depending on how its ends were held. What they needed to find was some new way of fixing one end and freeing the other, close enough to the cave's own way of holding it, to produce a note that would count, for the band's purposes, as evidence of the same discovery.

### 5.6 Rebinding the Cave

They worked through the following days largely apart from the rest of the band, who had mostly lost interest after the failed demonstration. Kythera abandoned the idea of hanging the stone loose at both ends, since that, she now believed, was exactly what could never sing the way the fixed stalactite had. Instead they tried holding one end rigid: wedging the thick base into a cleft between two rocks and packing it tight with wet clay left to dry hard around it, so that end could not shift at all, leaving only the narrow tip free to ring against open air the way the underground tip had rung against the chamber. Tak and the two who had come with him built a hollow structure around the free end from hide stretched over a bent-branch frame, an attempt to give the open air something like the chamber's enclosing effect, though none of them expected it to matter as much as getting the fixed end right.

They tuned it by trial across many failed attempts, packing the base tighter or looser, adjusting the depth of the hollow around the free tip, striking it after each change and listening for anything that resembled what Tak remembered from underground. They were not trying to rebuild the cave. They were trying to find some new way of fixing one end and freeing the other that would draw a related note out of the same stone, not the identical note, since the identical note had belonged to a connection that no longer existed anywhere but underground. Most attempts still produced only a dull knock. A few produced something thin and short-lived but unmistakably a note rather than a noise.

### 5.7 The Sound of Truth

When they finally brought the reconstruction before the band again, Tak did not promise them the cave. He told them plainly that what they were about to hear would not be identical to what he had heard in the ground, and asked them to judge only whether it was possible at all for a stone to sing this way, not whether it matched his original description exactly.

He struck it. The sound that came back was thinner than the chamber's had been, shorter, without the layered return that had unsettled him underground, but it rang, clearly and unmistakably, long enough that no one watching could call it an ordinary knock of stone. It was not the cave's voice. It was evidence that a voice like that could exist, built where everyone could see how it was built and could, if they wished, learn to build another one themselves.

### 5.8 Catharsis

What followed was not orderly. Some of the band stepped back from the stone as though it might do something further. Others pressed closer, wanting to strike it themselves and hear the note again under their own hand. A few began, almost without deciding to, moving their bodies to the rhythm Tak struck out, a kind of swaying that had no established form because nothing like it had asked their bodies to move this way before.

It did not stay Tak's discovery, or Kythera's reconstruction, for more than that first evening. By the second night others were building their own frames, hanging their own stones, arguing over lengths of cord and depths of hollow structure the way they might argue over the best angle to strike a blade. What had been one man's obsession and one woman's careful correction became, almost immediately, something that belonged to whoever wanted to make it belong to them.

### 5.9 What the Proof Destroyed

Tak went back to the cave once more, alone, some time after the reconstruction had already spread beyond anything he could still call his own. He found the gap in the ceiling where the stalactite had hung, a pale scar among formations that had grown undisturbed for longer than any person could reckon. He struck the neighboring stones, out of habit more than hope, and they still answered as they always had, but the space where his stone had been answered nothing at all.

He had wanted, more than anything, for the band to believe what he had found. They believed it now, more thoroughly than he could have arranged by any other means. What he had not fully understood, standing at the mouth of the passage seven years into his digging, was that believing him would cost the cave something it could not get back, a single voice among many, removed to make the rest of the voices credible.

### 5.10 The Chleomancer

In time the band gave him a name for what he had done, though it fit him no more comfortably than any of the earlier names had. They called him the one who commanded the singing stones, as though the cave's music had been his to command all along. He never corrected this in front of the others, but he knew, and Kythera knew, that the truth sat differently than the name suggested.

He had not commanded the cave. He had found it, failed to describe it, watched it refuse to travel in language, and finally learned, with help he could not have supplied alone, how to carry out enough of its relations, and lose enough of the rest, that someone standing far from that dark chamber could hear a version of what he had heard and recognize it as true. The cave still sang, wherever it still had voices left to sing with. What Tak had given the band was not the cave. It was the smallest working copy of it he had been able to build, and it was, in the end, the only version anyone but him would ever get to keep.
